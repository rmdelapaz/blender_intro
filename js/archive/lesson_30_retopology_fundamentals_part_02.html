✅ Good vs. Bad Topology

Recognizing quality topology is essential for effective retopology. You need to understand what you're aiming for before you start rebuilding meshes. Good topology isn't just aesthetic—it's functional, purposeful, and production-ready.

Topology Quality Criteria

📊 What Makes Topology "Good"

Good topology characteristics:

  • Quad dominant: 95%+ four-sided faces
    • Triangles only where necessary
    • No ngons (5+ sides)
    • Clean, predictable structure
  • Logical edge flow: Loops follow form and function
    • Circular loops around joints
    • Parallel loops along muscles
    • Flow continues without random termination
  • Even distribution: Faces roughly similar size
    • No tiny faces next to huge faces
    • Consistent density per area
    • Smooth subdivision results
  • Appropriate density: Detail where needed
    • High density: face, hands, deforming areas
    • Low density: torso, flat areas
    • Efficient polygon budget
  • Deformation-ready: Supports animation
    • Edge loops around joints
    • Follows anatomy
    • Bends naturally

Bad topology characteristics:

  • Random triangles throughout
  • Ngons (5+ sided faces)
  • Chaotic, unpredictable edge flow
  • Extreme variation in face sizes
  • No anatomical consideration
  • Subdivision creates artifacts

Edge Flow Patterns

🌊 Following the Forms

Face edge loops (circular):

  • Concentric loops around eyes
  • Loops around mouth opening
  • Loops around nose
  • Follow facial muscles and expressions
  • Critical for facial animation

Limb edge loops:

  • Circular loops around shoulders (like rings)
  • Circular loops around elbows
  • Circular loops around knees
  • Loops around wrists and ankles
  • Allow natural bending

Torso edge flow:

  • Loops around chest (follow ribcage)
  • Loops around waist
  • Vertical lines follow spine and torso form
  • Follow muscle structure

Extremities (hands/feet):

  • Loops around each finger/toe
  • Loops across palm/sole
  • Higher density for flexibility
  • Clean connection to arm/leg

Polygon Count Guidelines

🔢 Target Poly Counts

Character models (full body):

  • Mobile games: 1,000-5,000 triangles
    • Very low poly
    • Simple forms only
    • Heavy reliance on textures
  • Console/PC games (background): 5,000-15,000 triangles
    • Medium detail
    • Good for crowd characters
  • Console/PC games (hero): 15,000-50,000 triangles
    • High detail where needed
    • Main playable characters
    • Close-up shots
  • Film/animation: 10,000-100,000+ triangles
    • More flexibility
    • Depends on shot requirements
    • Subdivision adds detail

Remember: Quads vs. Triangles

  • Game engines count triangles
  • Artists work in quads
  • 1 quad = 2 triangles
  • 10,000 tri budget = ~5,000 quad model

Polygon density distribution:

  • Face: 25-35% of total budget
    • Most important, most detailed
    • Complex expressions need geometry
  • Hands: 10-15% of budget
    • Highly visible, articulated
    • Need detail for poses
  • Body: 40-50% of budget
    • Largest area
    • But less detail needed
  • Feet: 10-15% of budget
    • Often less visible than hands
    • Still need joint loops

Common Topology Mistakes

⚠️ What to Avoid

Poles in wrong places:

  • Problem: 3-pole or 5-pole in deforming area
  • Result: Pinching, artifacts when animated
  • Solution: Move poles to rigid areas
  • Acceptable in forehead, back of head, shoulders (rigid)
  • Bad in elbows, mouth, eyes (deforming)

Triangles throughout:

  • Problem: Scattered triangles everywhere
  • Result: Unpredictable subdivision
  • Solution: Convert to quads where possible
  • Few hidden triangles acceptable
  • Many visible triangles = amateur

Ngons (5+ sided faces):

  • Problem: Faces with more than 4 sides
  • Result: Subdivision artifacts, render issues
  • Solution: Subdivide into quads
  • Never leave ngons in final topology

No edge flow consideration:

  • Problem: Edges go random directions
  • Result: Can't select loops, bad deformation
  • Solution: Plan edge flow before modeling

Uneven face sizes:

  • Problem: Tiny faces next to huge faces
  • Result: Uneven subdivision, texturing issues
  • Solution: Keep faces roughly similar size

Too much/too little detail:

  • Problem: Million polys in torso, hundred in face
  • Result: Wasted polygons, poor deformation
  • Solution: Detail where needed most

💡 Topology Is Invisible But Critical: Users never see your topology. They see the rendered result. But topology determines everything about that result: how it moves, how it subdivides, how it performs, how it can be modified. Good topology is invisible. Bad topology creates visible problems. That's the paradox: you spend hours on something no one sees, so that they never see problems. Professional work requires professional topology. Not because clients inspect wireframes. But because good topology makes everything else work. Animation. Performance. Modification. Production. Master topology and you master the foundation of all 3D work.

🔄 The Retopology Workflow

Retopology follows a consistent workflow regardless of specific tools or techniques. Understanding this workflow helps you approach any retopology task systematically and efficiently.

Overview of the Process

📋 Step-by-Step Workflow

Phase 1: Preparation (10-15% of time)

  • Import or set up high-poly source mesh
  • Position at origin, apply transforms
  • Analyze forms and plan edge flow
  • Determine target polygon count
  • Reference anatomical diagrams if character

Phase 2: Blocking (20-30% of time)

  • Create major forms with low density
  • Establish primary edge loops
  • Focus on silhouette accuracy
  • Don't worry about details yet
  • Work quickly, iterate form

Phase 3: Refinement (40-50% of time)

  • Add edge loops for edge flow
  • Increase density in important areas
  • Perfect vertex placement
  • Ensure clean quads throughout
  • This is the bulk of the work

Phase 4: Verification (10-15% of time)

  • Test with Subdivision Surface modifier
  • Check deformation (if for animation)
  • Verify polygon count within budget
  • Look for topology errors
  • Final cleanup

Phase 5: Production Prep (10-15% of time)

  • UV unwrap the retopo mesh
  • Bake details from high to low
  • Set up materials with baked textures
  • Final presentation/delivery

Tools and Methods

🛠️ Retopology Approaches

Manual retopology:

  • Method: Build new mesh vertex by vertex
    • Complete control over every edge
    • Snap to source mesh surface
    • Place vertices precisely
  • Pros: Perfect control, best quality, learn deeply
  • Cons: Time-consuming, tedious
  • Best for: Characters, hero assets, learning

Automated/semi-automated:

  • Method: Algorithm creates topology
    • Quad Remesh (Blender 4.0+)
    • Instant Meshes (external tool)
    • ZRemesher (ZBrush)
  • Pros: Fast, good starting point
  • Cons: Less control, may need cleanup
  • Best for: Props, environment, backgrounds

Hybrid approach:

  • Method: Auto retopo + manual refinement
    • Automated tool creates base
    • Manual cleanup and optimization
    • Best of both worlds
  • Pros: Balance speed and quality
  • Cons: Still requires skill and time
  • Best for: Most production scenarios

Planning Your Retopology

🎯 Before You Start

Questions to answer first:

  • What's the intended use?
    • Game (which platform?)
    • Animation (deformation needs?)
    • Rendering only?
    • Use determines polygon budget
  • What's the polygon budget?
    • Strict limit (games) or flexible (film)?
    • Plan density distribution
    • Prioritize important areas
  • What needs to deform?
    • Full body animation?
    • Facial animation?
    • Static prop?
    • Determines edge loop requirements
  • Where is detail most important?
    • Face vs. body
    • Hero asset vs. background
    • Allocate polygons accordingly

Reference gathering:

  • Anatomy references: Muscle and bone structure
  • Topology references: Well-made similar models
  • Deformation references: How forms bend
  • Study before building

Sketch the plan:

  • Draw edge flow on screenshot
  • Plan major loops before starting
  • Identify where poles will go
  • 5 minutes planning saves hours fixing

Time Expectations

⏱️ How Long Does It Take?

Beginner retopologist:

  • Full character: 20-40 hours
    • Learning as you go
    • Many iterations and fixes
    • Normal for first attempts
  • Character head only: 8-12 hours
  • Simple prop: 2-4 hours

Intermediate retopologist:

  • Full character: 8-15 hours
    • Know the workflow
    • Fewer mistakes
    • Still careful and thorough
  • Character head only: 3-5 hours
  • Simple prop: 30-60 minutes

Professional retopologist:

  • Full character: 4-8 hours
    • Efficient workflow
    • Muscle memory
    • High quality, high speed
  • Character head only: 1-2 hours
  • Simple prop: 15-30 minutes

Speed increases with:

  • Practice volume (most important)
  • Tool familiarity (shortcuts, techniques)
  • Anatomical knowledge (know where loops go)
  • Planning skills (less backtracking)

💡 Retopology Is Meditation: Retopology is repetitive. Click, snap, extrude, repeat. For hours. Some find it tedious. Others find it meditative. Like knitting or painting—rhythmic, focused, satisfying. You zone into flow state. Music or podcasts help. The mesh grows vertex by vertex. Form emerges from chaos. Messy sculpt becomes clean geometry. There's something satisfying about that transformation. But here's the truth: whether you find it meditative or tedious doesn't matter. Because retopology is necessary. It's not optional. So you might as well develop techniques that make it bearable. Fast workflows. Good music. Efficient tools. Make peace with the process. Because every professional 3D artist spends time retopologizing. It's the price of perfect sculpts.
